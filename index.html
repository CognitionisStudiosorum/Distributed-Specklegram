Machine learning for turning an optical fibre specklegram sensor into a spatially resolved sensing system

A speckle sensor feels every touch along a multimode fibre, but not where it happened. Here a neural network learns to tell, from the speckle pattern at the far end alone, which of ten points along a 12 m fibre was hit.

Alberto Rodríguez-Cuevas, Marco Fontana, Luis Rodríguez-Cobo, Matías Lomer and José Miguel López-Higuera

Proof of concept. Manuscript JLT-21759-2018

A sensor that feels everything, everywhere

Shine coherent light into a multimode fibre and the far end shows a grainy pattern of bright and dark spots, a specklegram. It is extremely sensitive: a vibration, a change of temperature or a light touch anywhere along the fibre rearranges it. That makes the fibre specklegram sensor cheap and remarkably sensitive, and it has been used for non-contact patient monitoring and for intrusion and pedestrian detection.

The catch is that the sensor integrates. Every small contribution along the fibre ends up in the same pattern, so the whole fibre behaves as a single sensing point. Knowing that something happened is easy; knowing where is a question that had barely been addressed.

The hypothesis of this work is that the place of a perturbation leaves a consistent trace in how the pattern changes, even if that trace is far too tangled to model from first principles. If so, a classifier trained on many examples should be able to recover it.

Where the speckle comes from

A multimode fibre carries light along many paths at once, the modes. Each mode travels a slightly different optical length, so at the output they arrive with different phases and interfere. Where the phase delays add up to multiples of 2π the interference is constructive and a bright speckle appears; where they differ by π it is destructive and the spot is dark. Everything in between gives the grey levels in between.

Pressing the fibre changes the phases of the modes and the way they exchange energy, and the speckles move. A thinner core carries fewer modes and gives coarser speckles: with a numerical aperture typical of plastic fibre, around 0.5, a 50 µm core guides several thousand modes at 638 nm and a 240 µm core more than a hundred thousand.

From a hit to one image

Every hit lasts a little under a third of a second, and the camera records at 30 frames per second, so each hit is a nine-frame colour video. Three steps turn it into a single image that only describes how the pattern changed, not how hard or how fast the fibre was hit.

Keep only what changed

Each frame is converted to grey levels and the previous frame is subtracted, pixel by pixel. Speckles that did not move cancel; the ones that did remain.

One image per hit, independent of its strength

The difference frames inside a window of nine frames around the hit h are added up, and the result is divided by its own total. A strong and a gentle hit at the same point should then look alike, so the classifier has to rely on the shape of the change, which is what carries the position.

Two classifiers

A feedforward network with one hidden layer of 10 to 20 neurons serves as the reference: one input per pixel, one output per position. The second is a convolutional network of twelve layers: two blocks of convolution, max pooling and ReLU, then two fully connected layers and a softmax. Both were trained in Matlab R2016a on images of 60 × 80 pixels.

Ten positions, a thousand hits each

These are processed images from the dataset of Experiment II, 240 µm fibre, cycling through recorded hits at each position. To the eye they all look alike. The difference is statistical, and it is there.

The averages show something the paper did not look at. Hits close to the camera concentrate the change in the middle of the fibre core; hits far from it spread the change towards the edge. A natural reading is that a perturbation far from the output has more fibre to cross, and on the way its energy leaks into higher-order modes, which sit further from the axis. This is an observation on this dataset, not a result of the paper.

Results

Experiment I: three points, two fibres

A 6 m fibre was hit at three points 2 m apart, by the motor and by hand with a small stick or a fingertip, on two fibres: 50 and 240 µm. The 50 µm fibre is classified almost perfectly. The 240 µm fibre, with many more modes and smaller speckles, is harder: around 80 % in the larger test. It probably needs more data and a sharper camera.

First, feasibility tests on a few datasets, with images of 60 × 80 pixels (Fig. 4 of the paper). Rows are the true point, columns the point predicted, in percent.

Then a larger test (Tables 1 and 2 of the paper), with images from the same recording sessions in training and test, or from a session the network had never seen.

Hits by hand are harder, because no two are alike, yet they still reach 79 % among three points: the pre-processing does stop the networks from relying on how hard or how fast the fibre was hit. Training on some sessions and testing on another costs accuracy, because the pattern drifts with temperature and alignment from one day to the next.

Experiment II: ten points along 12 m

The harder fibre, 240 µm, was then laid along 12 m and hit by the motor at ten points 1 m apart, with the set-up untouched during the whole recording.

Mistakes are almost always a neighbouring point, and the further apart two points are, the less often they are confused. And accuracy climbs towards the camera: positions 9 and 10 are recognised 88 to 100 % of the time, positions 1 to 6 often less than half. Perturbations are known to fade as light travels on, so a hit near the output reaches the camera with less of the fibre to blur it.

What it shows

With a basic set-up, a camera and a standard classifier, a single multimode fibre can tell where it was touched: up to 99 % among three points and 71 % among ten. The pattern of errors says the information is genuinely about distance, not about the shape of the hit. The system is also fragile: any change in the environment or in the alignment between fibre and camera degrades it, and that is the main thing to solve before applications.

The obvious next steps are larger datasets, higher camera resolution for fibres with many modes, other learning methods, and focusing the analysis on the regions of the specklegram that carry the most information, such as the radial structure visible in the averages above. Later work has made the same point from the physical side: spatially resolved sensing in multimode fibres depends on mode coupling that changes along the fibre (Smith et al., Photonics Research 2024).
